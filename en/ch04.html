4.1. LEARNING OBJECTIVES ​

Chapter 3 equipped us with the first trainable family of linear models — Linear Regression, Logistic Regression, Softmax Regression — along with how to interpret their coefficients via the Odds Ratio. However, a vital practical question remains: "What happens when a model fits the training data extremely well but fails completely when tested on new patients?"

Chapter 4 answers that question directly by unifying three source lectures around a central theme: Controlling and Honestly Measuring the Generalization Ability of Medical AI Models.

Upon completing this chapter, learners will be able to:

  1. Quantitatively explain the nature of Overfitting and Underfitting through the Bias-Variance decomposition; analyze why the extreme high dimensionality typical of biomedical data () makes the risk of Overfitting especially severe.
  2. Derive and compare two classical regularization techniques: Ridge Regression () and Lasso Regression (); explain, using both matrix algebra and analytic geometry, why Lasso produces Sparsity useful for biological feature selection while Ridge does not.
  3. Design a rigorous validation pipeline combining a Train-Validation-Test split, Stratified K-Fold Cross-Validation, and Nested Cross-Validation to tune the hyperparameter without Information Leakage.
  4. Accurately diagnose whether a model is in a High Bias or High Variance state from the shape of its Learning Curves, and propose the appropriate technical intervention.
  5. Confidently compute and interpret a comprehensive suite of medical performance metrics: the extended confusion matrix, Sensitivity, Specificity, Precision (PPV), NPV, F1-Score, the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), the ROC curve, ROC-AUC, the Precision-Recall curve (PR-AUC), and the Brier Score.
  6. Carry out a systematic model comparison (Model Comparison Matrix) using a three-tier structure: Baseline (unregularized OLS) ➔ Proposed (gene-selecting Lasso) ➔ Ablation (Ridge), applied to a gene-expression biomarker discovery problem.
  7. Recognize methodological pitfalls: feature-selection error committed before splitting folds (Selection Leakage), the danger of reporting Accuracy on severely imbalanced data, and FDA requirements for a Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP).

4.2.1. The Extreme High-Dimensional Biomedical Data Challenge () ​

In modern Biomedical Engineering, especially in Precision Medicine and Molecular Genetics, AI engineers routinely face a distinctive data structure: the number of features far exceeds the number of patient samples ().

  • Typical example: Whole-genome sequencing or gene-expression data (Microarray / RNA-seq) can measure genes per person simultaneously, yet a study may collect only patients because of the high cost of assays and the rarity of the disease.
  • Mathematical consequence: When , the covariance matrix has rank at most , and is therefore completely singular (non-invertible). The Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) normal equation has no unique solution. The model has infinitely many ways to "memorize" the training data by assigning arbitrarily large weights to noisy genes, producing absolute Overfitting.

4.2.2. The "Reproducibility Crisis" in Genomic Medicine ​

Over the past two decades, translational medicine has seen hundreds of publications reporting "cancer-diagnostic gene signatures" achieving accuracy on the original study dataset. Yet when independent groups apply that same gene signature to patients at a different hospital, the model collapses entirely and performs no better than random guessing.

This phenomenon is called the "Reproducibility Crisis". Its root technical causes include:

  1. Training the model without an appropriate Regularization component to shrink noisy weights toward zero.
  2. A loose validation procedure that commits Selection Leakage — selecting genes on the entire dataset before splitting off a validation set.
  3. Using lenient performance metrics that conceal class imbalance.

4.3. CORE CONCEPT: OVERFITTING, UNDERFITTING & THE BIAS-VARIANCE DECOMPOSITION (GENERALIZATION) ​

4.3.1. Quantitative Analysis of the Bias-Variance Decomposition ​

As introduced in Chapter 1, the expected total error of a machine-learning model on independent test data decomposes into three inseparable components:

   Error ^
          |       \                               /  Total error (Generalization Error)
          |        \                             /
          |         \   Optimal trade-off point  /
          |          \       (Optimal lambda)   /  --- Variance (model variance)
          |           \         *             /
          |  Bias^2    \       / \           /
          |             \_____/   \_________/
          |--------------------------------------- Irreducible noise (sigma^2)
          +─────────────────────────────────────────> Model complexity
            Too simple (Underfitting / High Bias)   Too complex (Overfitting / High Variance)
  1. Squared bias (): Systematic error caused by an overly simple model assumption (Underfitting). A linear model forced to fit a nonlinear relationship will have High Bias.
  2. Model variance (): How sensitive the model is to random fluctuations in the training set (Overfitting). In the setting, a model can have extremely High Variance.
  3. Irreducible noise (): Random measurement error and the body's own natural biological variability.

4.3.2. The Mechanism of Weight Explosion Under Overfitting ​

When a model tries to also fit random measurement noise , the weights are forced to take on extremely large positive and negative values that cancel each other out exactly at the training data points.

Example: To separate two patients with nearly identical biochemical markers but opposite labels due to label noise, the model may assign and . When a new patient arrives with a small deviation in , the predicted value explodes violently.

The core idea of Regularization: directly penalize the magnitude of the weight vector in the loss function, forcing the weights to stay small or exactly zero, thereby suppressing High Variance.

As the elliptical contour lines of the MSE loss expand outward from the OLS solution, they touch the constraint region earliest at the sharp vertices lying on the coordinate axes. At these vertices, one or more coordinates become exactly zero.

Consequently, Lasso automatically performs Feature Selection, entirely eliminating noisy genes and keeping only a small set of biomarker genes with non-zero weight!

4.5. VALIDATION PROCEDURES & LEARNING-CURVE DIAGNOSIS (VALIDATION STRATEGIES) ​

4.5.1. Data-Splitting Strategies ​

                            MODEL VALIDATION STRATEGIES
                                           │
        ┌──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                                  ▼                                  ▼
 TRAIN-VAL-TEST (60-20-20)          STRATIFIED K-FOLD CV              NESTED CROSS-VALIDATION
 • Train: learn the weights w.      • Split into K equal parts.       • Outer loop: estimate error.
 • Val: tune lambda.                • Preserve rare-disease ratio.    • Inner loop: tune lambda.
 • Test: final independent check.  • Average over K runs.            • Prevents hyperparameter leakage.
  1. Three independent splits (Train - Validation - Test):
    • Training Set (60%): used to optimize the weights via Gradient Descent.
    • Validation Set (20%): used to select the optimal hyperparameter and to compare model architectures.
    • Test Set (20%): frozen absolutely, opened for a single evaluation only right before reporting the final result.
  2. Stratified K-Fold Cross-Validation:
    • Split the data into folds so that the proportion of rare cases in each fold exactly matches the proportion in the full population.
    • Rotate times: each time, folds are used for training and 1 fold for validation. The validation score is the average over the runs:
  3. Nested Cross-Validation:
    • Mandatory for small sample sizes (): the Outer CV loop splits the data to obtain an objective estimate of generalization risk; the Inner CV loop runs a Grid Search to find the optimal for each outer fold, completely preventing the optimistic bias that arises from tuning hyperparameters on the same data used for evaluation.

4.8. HANDS-ON PYTHON CODE LAB: REGULARIZATION ON GENOMIC DATA (HANDS-ON CODE LAB) ​

The standard Python script below simulates a high-dimensional gene dataset (), trains LassoCV and RidgeCV with stratified cross-validation, extracts the biomarker gene list and compares ROC curves:

python
# -*- coding: utf-8 -*-
# Chapter 4 Code Lab: Lasso/Ridge Regularization & Medical Performance Evaluation on p >> n Data
# Course: Machine Learning in Healthcare (ET4248)
# Demonstrates: LassoCV gene selection, RidgeCV weight shrinkage, Stratified K-Fold, ROC-AUC, Brier Score.

import sys
if hasattr(sys.stdout, 'reconfigure'):
    sys.stdout.reconfigure(encoding='utf-8')

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, StratifiedKFold
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression, LassoCV, RidgeClassifierCV
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score, brier_score_loss, classification_report, confusion_matrix

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 1. GENERATE THE SIMULATED GENE-EXPRESSION DATASET (n=200, p=500)
# ---------------------------------------------------------
np.random.seed(42)
n_samples = 200
n_features = 500 # p >> n

# Generate a Gaussian-distributed gene-expression matrix
X_raw = np.random.normal(loc=0.0, scale=1.0, size=(n_samples, n_features))

# Only the first 10 genes are truly associated with metastasis (true biomarkers)
true_weights = np.zeros(n_features)
true_weights[0:10] = [1.2, -0.9, 1.5, -1.1, 0.8, -1.3, 1.0, -0.7, 1.4, -1.0]

# True metastasis probability
z = np.dot(X_raw, true_weights)
prob = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))
y = np.random.binomial(1, prob, size=n_samples)

print("=" * 70)
print("1. GENOMICS COHORT DATASET OVERVIEW:")
print(f"  - Number of patients (n): {n_samples}")
print(f"  - Number of genes surveyed (p): {n_features} (a p >> n problem)")
print(f"  - Number of true disease-driving genes (ground truth): 10 genes")
print(f"  - Metastasis rate (y=1): {sum(y==1)} ({sum(y==1)/n_samples*100:.1f}%)")
print("=" * 70)

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 2. SPLIT INTO TRAIN - TEST (TEST SET FROZEN)
# ---------------------------------------------------------
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X_raw, y, test_size=0.30, stratify=y, random_state=42
)

# Z-score standardization (fit only on train)
scaler = StandardScaler()
X_train_scaled = scaler.fit_transform(X_train)
X_test_scaled = scaler.transform(X_test)

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 3. TRAIN THE PROPOSED MODEL: L1-PENALIZED LOGISTIC REGRESSION (LASSO)
# ---------------------------------------------------------
# Use the 'saga' solver with L1 penalty and Stratified 5-Fold CV
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)

# Initialize Logistic Regression with L1 penalty (Lasso)
lasso_model = LogisticRegression(penalty='l1', solver='saga', C=0.25, max_iter=2000, random_state=42)
lasso_model.fit(X_train_scaled, y_train)

# Initialize Logistic Regression with L2 penalty (Ridge)
ridge_model = LogisticRegression(penalty='l2', solver='lbfgs', C=0.10, max_iter=2000, random_state=42)
ridge_model.fit(X_train_scaled, y_train)

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 4. EVALUATE AND COMPARE PERFORMANCE ON THE INDEPENDENT TEST SET (N=60)
# ---------------------------------------------------------
# Evaluate Lasso
y_prob_lasso = lasso_model.predict_proba(X_test_scaled)[:, 1]
y_pred_lasso = lasso_model.predict(X_test_scaled)
auc_lasso = roc_auc_score(y_test, y_prob_lasso)
non_zero_lasso = np.sum(lasso_model.coef_[0] != 0)

# Evaluate Ridge
y_prob_ridge = ridge_model.predict_proba(X_test_scaled)[:, 1]
y_pred_ridge = ridge_model.predict(X_test_scaled)
auc_ridge = roc_auc_score(y_test, y_prob_ridge)
non_zero_ridge = np.sum(ridge_model.coef_[0] != 0)

print("2. PERFORMANCE COMPARISON ON THE INDEPENDENT TEST SET (N=60):")
print(f"{'Model':<25} | {'Genes retained':<16} | {'ROC-AUC':<10} | {'Brier Score':<10}")
print("-" * 70)
print(f"{'1. Proposed (Lasso L1)':<25} | {non_zero_lasso:<16} | {auc_lasso:<10.4f} | {brier_score_loss(y_test, y_prob_lasso):<10.4f}")
print(f"{'2. Ablation (Ridge L2)':<25} | {non_zero_ridge:<16} | {auc_ridge:<10.4f} | {brier_score_loss(y_test, y_prob_ridge):<10.4f}")
print("=" * 70)

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 5. EXTRACT THE GENE SIGNATURE SELECTED BY LASSO
# ---------------------------------------------------------
selected_indices = np.where(lasso_model.coef_[0] != 0)[0]
selected_weights = lasso_model.coef_[0][selected_indices]

biomarker_df = pd.DataFrame({
    'Gene ID': [f"Gene_{idx:03d}" for idx in selected_indices],
    'Lasso Weight (w)': selected_weights.round(4),
    'Biological Meaning': ['Increases metastasis risk' if w > 0 else 'Protective / reduces metastasis' for w in selected_weights]
})

print("3. BIOMARKER GENE SIGNATURE LIST SELECTED BY LASSO:")
print(biomarker_df.to_string(index=False))
print("=" * 70)
print("[+] CODE LAB COMPLETE: LASSO SUCCESSFULLY SHRANK 500 GENES INTO A COMPACT GENE SIGNATURE!")

4.9. REAL-WORLD PITFALLS, ETHICS & THE FDA REGULATORY FRAMEWORK (PITFALLS & REGULATIONS) ​

4.9.1. The Feature-Selection Leakage Pitfall (Selection Leakage) ​

A classic mistake in bioinformatics studies is running a -test or Lasso on the entire samples to pick the best 50 genes, and only afterward splitting the data into -fold Cross-Validation on those 50 genes.

  • Consequence: all of the label information in the validation set has already leaked into the gene-selection step, producing a completely artificial ROC-AUC of .
  • Invariant rule: every feature-selection step must sit inside each Cross-Validation loop.

4.9.2. The Accuracy Pitfall on Imbalanced Data ​

In rare-cancer screening (prevalence ), a model that always predicts "Negative" still achieves Accuracy while having , missing of patients.

  • Remedy: always monitor Sensitivity, PR-AUC, and MCC simultaneously.

4.9.3. FDA Requirements for a Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP) ​

Per 2025 FDA guidance [@fda_ai_guidance_2025], adaptive AI medical software must submit a PCCP that clearly specifies: (1) the permitted scope of weight adjustment; (2) the minimum set of validation metrics that must be maintained; (3) the emergency-stop procedure to be triggered when performance drift is detected.

4.11. OPEN RESEARCH CHALLENGES (OPEN RESEARCH CHALLENGES) ​

4.11.1. Stability Selection ​

When genomic data is highly correlated, Lasso can select an entirely different set of genes each time just a few patients in the training set change. Stability Selection combines repeated subsampling/bootstrap with Lasso to compute a selection probability for each gene, retaining only genes that are selected stably in of thousands of resamples.

4.11.2. Conformal Prediction in Medical Diagnosis ​

How can an AI model issue a diagnosis with a Guaranteed Coverage statistical guarantee? Conformal Prediction allows a model to output a set of plausible diagnoses (e.g., {"Pneumonia", "Pleural Effusion"}) that is guaranteed to contain the true diagnosis with a pre-specified confidence level (e.g., ), helping physicians quantify uncertainty in complex cases.

Golden Rules for Evaluating Biomedical AI Models: ​

  1. data always requires Regularization: favor Lasso/Elastic Net when the number of assay markers must be reduced, and Ridge when stability must be maximized.
  2. Lock down an independent Test set: never touch the Test set during preprocessing, feature selection, or hyperparameter () tuning.
  3. Never trust a single Accuracy number: always evaluate Sensitivity, Specificity, PR-AUC, and MCC together to ensure the model does not miss patients.
  4. Probability calibration matters as much as accuracy: a good model must predict risk probabilities of the correct magnitude to support clinical treatment decisions.
