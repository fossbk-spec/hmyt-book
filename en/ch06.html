6.1. LEARNING OBJECTIVES ​

All chapters from Chapter 1 through Chapter 5 were built on one prerequisite assumption: every patient sample comes with an accurate target label . In clinical practice, however, most of the enormous volume of biomedical data collected from continuous monitoring devices, gene sequencing, and electronic health records (EHR) is entirely unlabeled.

Chapter 6 equips learners with the methodology of Unsupervised Learning to automatically discover hidden structure, stratify disease phenotypes (Clinical Phenotyping), and compress/separate complex biomedical signal sources.

After completing this chapter, learners will be able to:

  1. Clearly distinguish the nature of Supervised vs. Unsupervised Learning; analyze the practical barriers (expert cost, follow-up time, diagnostic uncertainty) that leave medical data without gold-standard labels.
  2. Formalize and derive the K-Means clustering algorithm: from the Distortion Function, the two-step iterative procedure (Assignment / Update), the K-Means++ initialization algorithm, to its inherent geometric limitations (the convex-spherical-cluster assumption).
  3. Build the probabilistic Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM): derive the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm, explain the concept of soft assignment (Responsibility ), and distinguish the different covariance matrix structures.
  4. Correctly apply internal cluster-validation metrics: the Elbow Method, the Silhouette Coefficient , and Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC / AIC) to scientifically select the optimal number of clusters .
  5. Mathematically derive Principal Component Analysis (PCA): from two equivalent viewpoints (maximizing the variance of the projected data and minimizing the squared reconstruction error); perform eigendecomposition on the covariance matrix.
  6. Master the Blind Source Separation (BSS) model of Independent Component Analysis (ICA): distinguish between PCA's orthogonality/linear-uncorrelatedness and ICA's higher-order, non-Gaussian statistical independence (via Kurtosis and Negentropy).
  7. Design an unlabeled model-comparison pipeline (Internal Model Comparison) on two classic clinical problems: clinical phenotype stratification of sleep apnea (OSA Phenotyping via K-Means/GMM) and non-invasive fetal ECG source separation (fECG Extraction via ICA).
  8. Avoid common methodological pitfalls: the mandatory standardization requirement, the data-leakage error of fitting PCA/KMeans on the entire pooled dataset, the singular-covariance-matrix phenomenon in GMM, and the harmful misconception that "PCA can fully replace Feature Selection."

6.2. CLINICAL MOTIVATION: PHENOTYPE STRATIFICATION & BIOSIGNAL SOURCE SEPARATION ​

6.2.1. Clinical Phenotyping ​

In Precision Medicine, traditional diagnostic labels such as "Type 2 Diabetes," "Asthma," or "Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA)" are in fact not a single homogeneous disease, but a collection of many small disease subtypes (Subtypes / Phenotypes) with entirely different pathophysiological mechanisms and treatment responses (Arjun Panesar, 2019 [@panesar2019]).

             THE UNIVARIATE PARADOX IN OSA CLINICAL CLASSIFICATION

  Patient A: AHI = 30 ───> Severe daytime sleepiness, obesity, mechanical obstruction ──> CPAP responder
  Patient B: AHI = 30 ───> Nighttime insomnia, anxiety, poor respiratory-muscle activation ────> CPAP intolerant

  => Looking only at the univariate AHI = 30, a physician would treat both the same way and fail!
  => Multidimensional Clustering is needed to discover the hidden Clinical Phenotypes.
  • The Univariate Paradox: Traditional OSA severity diagnosis relies on a single index, the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI, events/hour**)**. Both Patient A and Patient B have (severe grade), yet Patient A has daytime sleepiness from mechanical airway obstruction (very responsive to CPAP positive-pressure therapy), while Patient B has insomnia and nighttime awakenings from neural control dysregulation (CPAP-intolerant).
  • The Unsupervised-Learning Solution: Multidimensional clustering over the set of symptom indices, anatomical structure, and polysomnography automatically separates the hidden phenotypes, opening the way to targeted treatment.

6.2.2. Data Compression & Non-Invasive Biosignal Source Separation ​

Many modern biomedical data-acquisition systems (64-channel EEG, -gene sequencing, fMRI) produce enormous data spaces with severe redundancy across measurement channels:

  1. Data Compression (PCA): Compress thousands of tightly correlated dimensions into a small number of Principal Components that represent of the information variance without losing the core structure.
  2. Blind Source Separation (ICA): In obstetric monitoring, electrodes placed on the mother's abdomen pick up a mixed electrical signal consisting of: the maternal ECG (mECG - large amplitude), the fetal ECG (fECG - very small amplitude), and abdominal-muscle noise. The physician cannot know the mixing ratio in advance. ICA makes it possible to completely and non-invasively separate the fetal heart rhythm from the maternal heart rhythm automatically.

6.3.2. The K-Means++ Initialization Algorithm ​

Random initialization of the starting centroids can trap K-Means in a poor-quality local minimum. The K-Means++ algorithm improves the initialization step:

  1. Randomly select the first centroid from the dataset.
  2. For each point , compute the shortest distance to the already-chosen centroids.
  3. Select the next centroid from a probability distribution proportional to the squared distance:
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until centroids are obtained. The algorithm guarantees the initial centroids are maximally spread apart in the space.

6.3.3. Inherent Limitations of K-Means in Healthcare ​

  • The homogeneous convex-sphere assumption: K-Means can only find clusters that are symmetric spheres of comparable size; it completely fails on elongated ellipsoidal clusters or clusters of uneven density.
  • The rigidity of Hard Assignment: A case sitting right on the boundary between two disease subtypes must be assigned to one cluster, losing clinically meaningful uncertainty information.

6.7.2. The Three Core Assumptions of ICA ​

  1. Statistical Independence: The biological signal sources emitted by different organs are fully statistically independent: .
  2. Non-Gaussianity: At most 1 source is allowed to follow a Gaussian distribution. (If there were 2 Gaussian sources, the joint distribution would be spherically symmetric, making it impossible to uniquely identify the separating axes.)

By the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), the sum of independent random variables is always "more Gaussian" than any individual component variable. Therefore, to separate sources, ICA searches for a direction such that the recovered signal achieves maximum non-Gaussianity.

6.8.2. Scenario 2: Fetal ECG Extraction (fECG Extraction) via FastICA ​

4-channel bioelectric signal acquisition from a pregnant patient's abdominal wall. The raw signal is dominated by the maternal ECG (mECG amplitude ) and muscle noise. In a full clinical deployment, FastICA can separate up to 4 independent sources corresponding to the 4 acquisition channels:

  • Source 1: Maternal heart rhythm (, non-Gaussian distribution).
  • Source 2: Fetal heart rhythm (, sharply visible fetal R-wave peaks).
  • Source 3: Maternal respiratory noise.
  • Source 4: power-line noise.
  • Applying PCA: The PC1 axis captures only the maternal waveform entirely; the subsequent axes remain a confused mixture of fetal QRS and maternal noise.
  • Applying FastICA: Fully separates the sources listed above; the code lab executed in §6.9 illustrates the core mechanism using a reduced 2-source version (mECG and fECG, 2 mixed channels) to keep the source code easy to follow — the mathematical principle extends directly to the full 4-source/4-channel case.

6.8.3. Scenario 3: Heart Failure Phenotype Stratification by Ejection Fraction (HFrEF/HFmrEF/HFpEF) ​

Classifying heart failure by Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (lvef_pct, LVEF) is the most classic phenotype-stratification system in modern cardiology, and the one with the most direct treatment implications — fundamentally different from the 3 cardiovascular-event groups in the Chapter 3 case study (10-year risk prediction): here the task is to stratify patients who already have heart failure into distinct pathophysiological subgroups:

Phenotype GroupLVEF ThresholdKey PathophysiologyClinical Significance
HFrEF (Heart failure with reduced EF)Predominant systolic dysfunction, dilated left ventricle.Full indication for the "four-pillar" regimen (ARNI, beta-blocker, MRA, SGLT2i) — strongest RCT evidence.
HFmrEF (Heart failure with mildly reduced EF)Transitional zone, pathophysiology mixed between HFrEF and HFpEF.Consider partial four-pillar therapy according to individual response.
HFpEF (Heart failure with preserved EF)Predominant diastolic dysfunction, thick/stiff left ventricle but normal contraction.Commonly seen in elderly patients with hypertension and diabetes; responds poorly to classic regimens, prioritize SGLT2i and comorbidity control.

In machine-learning terms, this is exactly the clustering problem on a multidimensional feature space (LVEF, NT-proBNP, age, hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation) that K-Means/GMM learned in §6.3–6.4 — the difference from §6.8.1 (OSA) is that here the LVEF boundary has already been standardized as a hard threshold by international literature, so GMM with covariance_type='full' is better suited than K-Means for modeling the blurry HFmrEF transitional zone, similar to how GMM handled the overlapping-phenotype cases in the OSA case study. Implementation directly reuses the pipeline already built in §6.9 (Steps 3–4), only swapping the input feature set for the heart-failure variables above.

6.8.4. Scenario 4: Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria Endotype Stratification (CSU Type I / Type IIb) ​

Similar to HFrEF/HFpEF, Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria (CSU) is also stratified into endotypes based on immunological biomarkers — but with an important difference: the boundary between groups has no internationally standardized hard threshold like LVEF, making this a "purer" unsupervised clustering problem than HFrEF/HFpEF.

  • Input features: total IgE, ASST (Autologous Serum Skin Test) result, BAT (Basophil Activation Test) result, Anti-TPO concentration, peripheral blood eosinophil/basophil counts.
  • Expected clusters: 3 known clinical groups — CSU Type I (high IgE, ASST/BAT negative), CSU Type IIb (ASST/BAT positive, high Anti-TPO, reduced eosinophils/basophils), and an Overlap group (positive for both mechanisms).
  • Why this is better suited to GMM than K-Means: The boundary between the 3 groups is not clearly spherically separated (especially the Overlap group sitting between the other two), requiring full covariance modeling and soft assignment — exactly the kind of problem GMM handles better than K-Means, as analyzed in §6.3.3 and §6.4.1.

Methodological note: Unlike HFrEF/HFpEF (where the threshold is already consensus in the literature), the "correctness" of a CSU endotype clustering cannot be judged purely by internal metrics like Silhouette or BIC — it must be cross-validated against real treatment response (Omalizumab) as an external validation criterion, an important limitation of unsupervised learning when applied to disease subtypes for which the literature has no unified "gold-standard label."

# Reduction & FastICA Source Separation

# Runs: Silhouette Analysis, K-Means, GMM with BIC, PCA compression and FastICA signal separation.

import sys
if hasattr(sys.stdout, 'reconfigure'):
    sys.stdout.reconfigure(encoding='utf-8')

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA, FastICA
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 1. BUILD A SIMULATED OSA CLINICAL PHENOTYPE DATASET (N=300 PATIENTS)
# ---------------------------------------------------------
np.random.seed(42)
n_samples = 300

# Generate 3 latent OSA clinical phenotypes (Ground Truth Phenotypes)
# Group 1: Obese - Severe obstructive sleep apnea (Obese OSA)
g1 = np.random.multivariate_normal(mean=[34, 45, 16, 78], cov=np.diag([4, 25, 4, 16]), size=100)
# Group 2: Insomnia - Mild/moderate sleep apnea (Insomnia OSA)
g2 = np.random.multivariate_normal(mean=[24, 18, 6, 88], cov=np.diag([3, 16, 3, 9]), size=100)
# Group 3: Elderly - Mixed sleep apnea (Elderly OSA)
g3 = np.random.multivariate_normal(mean=[27, 30, 11, 82], cov=np.diag([3, 20, 3, 12]), size=100)

X_raw = np.vstack([g1, g2, g3])
feature_names = ['BMI', 'AHI', 'Epworth_Score', 'Min_SpO2']

df = pd.DataFrame(X_raw, columns=feature_names)
print("=" * 70)
print("1. OVERVIEW OF THE SLEEP APNEA (OSA) CLINICAL COHORT:")
print(df.head())
print("=" * 70)

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 2. MANDATORY SCALE STANDARDIZATION (Z-SCORE)
# ---------------------------------------------------------
scaler = StandardScaler()
X_scaled = scaler.fit_transform(X_raw)

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 3. INTERNAL EVALUATION TO FIND THE OPTIMAL CLUSTER COUNT (SILHOUETTE & BIC)
# ---------------------------------------------------------
print("2. SILHOUETTE AND BIC ANALYSIS TO SELECT THE NUMBER OF CLUSTERS K:")
silhouette_scores = []
bic_scores = []
k_range = range(2, 6)

for k in k_range:
    # K-Means Silhouette
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, init='k-means++', n_init=10, random_state=42)
    labels = km.fit_predict(X_scaled)
    s_score = silhouette_score(X_scaled, labels)
    silhouette_scores.append(s_score)

    # GMM BIC
    gmm = GaussianMixture(n_components=k, covariance_type='full', random_state=42)
    gmm.fit(X_scaled)
    bic_scores.append(gmm.bic(X_scaled))

    print(f"  - K = {k}: Silhouette Score = {s_score:.4f} | GMM BIC = {gmm.bic(X_scaled):.1f}")

best_k = k_range[np.argmax(silhouette_scores)]
print(f"[+] The optimal number of clusters by Silhouette is: K = {best_k}")
print("-" * 70)

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 4. TRAIN K-MEANS & GMM AT THE OPTIMAL K
# ---------------------------------------------------------
kmeans_final = KMeans(n_clusters=best_k, init='k-means++', n_init=10, random_state=42)
df['KMeans_Cluster'] = kmeans_final.fit_predict(X_scaled)

gmm_final = GaussianMixture(n_components=best_k, covariance_type='full', random_state=42)
gmm_final.fit(X_scaled)
probs = gmm_final.predict_proba(X_scaled)
df['GMM_Cluster'] = gmm_final.predict(X_scaled)
df['GMM_Max_Prob'] = np.max(probs, axis=1).round(3)

print("3. MEAN CHARACTERISTICS OF THE CLINICAL PHENOTYPES (K-MEANS CLUSTERS):")
cluster_summary = df.groupby('KMeans_Cluster')[feature_names].mean().round(2)
print(cluster_summary)
print("=" * 70)

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 5. DIMENSIONALITY REDUCTION VIA PRINCIPAL COMPONENT ANALYSIS (PCA)
# ---------------------------------------------------------
pca = PCA(n_components=2)
X_pca = pca.fit_transform(X_scaled)

evr = pca.explained_variance_ratio_
print("4. PCA DIMENSIONALITY-REDUCTION RESULTS:")
print(f"  - Explained variance ratio, PC1 axis: {evr[0]*100:.2f}%")
print(f"  - Explained variance ratio, PC2 axis: {evr[1]*100:.2f}%")
print(f"  - Total cumulative variance, first 2 axes: {sum(evr)*100:.2f}% (Preserves > 85% of the information)")
print("-" * 70)

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 6. SEPARATE A SIMULATED MIXED BIOSIGNAL VIA FASTICA

# Create 2 synthetic signal sources: maternal heart rhythm (periodic sine wave) and fetal

6.10. PRACTICAL PITFALLS, ETHICS & METHODOLOGICAL CONSTRAINTS ​

6.10.1. Mandatory Scale Standardization Before Clustering & PCA ​

All the algorithms in this chapter depend strictly on Euclidean distance or the covariance matrix. Without Z-score standardization, a variable with naturally large variance (such as Forced Vital Capacity FVC in , values of ) will dominate of the PC1 direction and the K-Means distances, completely neutralizing other important biochemical indices.

6.10.2. The Data-Leakage Pitfall of Fitting PCA on the Whole Pooled Dataset ​

A common error in published medical AI work: load all patients, run pca.fit_transform(), and only afterward split into Train and Test to train a classifier.

  • Consequence: The learned principal-component axes have absorbed the variance distribution of the Test set as well, causing Data Leakage.
  • Rule: PCA must only be fit on the Train set; then use transform on the Test set.

6.10.3. The Singular Covariance Matrix Phenomenon in GMM ​

When a cluster contains only a very small number of tightly clustered patients, or the features have perfect multicollinearity, the matrix has a determinant approaching 0 (). The density function then blows up to , causing the EM algorithm to collapse from a division-by-zero error.

  • Fix: Add a small regularization term to the diagonal, reg_covar=1e-6, in GaussianMixture.

6.10.4. The Misconception That "PCA Can Fully Replace Feature Selection" ​

PCA creates new axes that are linear combinations of all the original variables.

  • In clinical diagnosis, a physician cannot explain: "The patient is critical because the index exceeds the threshold."
  • If the goal is to find biomarkers that can be directly measured in the lab, Feature Selection (Lasso/RFE) must be used instead of PCA.

Golden Rules for Applying Unsupervised Learning in Healthcare: ​

  1. Always apply Z-score standardization: Distance and variance are completely distorted if the test indices are not brought onto the same scale.
  2. Never rely on a single metric to choose : Always cross-check the Silhouette Coefficient, the Elbow chart, and consult medical experts on the biological plausibility of the clusters found.
  3. Clearly distinguish the purposes of PCA and ICA: Use PCA when dimensionality compression is needed; use ICA when truly independent biological signal sources need to be separated.
  4. Never fit a transform on the whole pooled dataset: Strictly follow the rule of fitting only on the Train set to prevent data leakage into the Test set.
